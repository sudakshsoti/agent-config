# Lenses and standards

Read this when diagnosing a draft and you want the exact when-to-apply trigger and the test for each lens, or the full standards checklist.

## The four lenses

Name the writer in the diagnosis when a draft hits the trigger. The lens tells him what kind of fix the rewrite will apply.

### Munro — compression and emotional economy
Apply when: a draft overexplains, narrates feelings instead of showing them, or runs long on interior monologue.
Test: can the paragraph be half as long and twice as felt?
Fix in the rewrite: cut to a single gesture; let the reader infer the feeling.

### Burkeman — philosophy landing in daily life
Apply when: he's abstract, writing about "the human condition", or generalising.
Test: is there a specific weekday, room, or object the idea touches down on?
Fix in the rewrite: anchor the idea to a concrete moment. A Tuesday afternoon, not the human condition.

### Chayka — ordinary things taken seriously
Apply when: he dismisses a subject as too small or too domestic, or treats it apologetically.
Test: is the ordinary subject being taken seriously as a subject?
Fix in the rewrite: commit to the small subject; the smallness was never the problem, the hedging was.

### Bennett — dry observational warmth
Apply when: he overexplains a feeling, or the ending tries to summarise.
Test: is the warmth showing in the observation, or being announced?
Fix in the rewrite: end on the throwaway line that carries the weight; trust the reader.

## The six standards

Use as the checklist behind the diagnosis and the bar for the rewrite. Name the principle when something fails it.

1. Specificity over generality. At least one image, object, or moment the reader can see. If you can't picture it, it isn't earning the page.
2. Emotional honesty without sentimentality. Say what you mean. Performed vulnerability reads as fake even when the feeling is real.
3. Earned complexity. Start concrete, let abstraction emerge. Never open on the thesis.
4. Tonal control. One person talking, not "writing". A sentence that sounds like a Medium post is one.
5. Endings that resonate, not conclude. No bow. End mid-gesture or on a dry observation that isn't throwaway.
6. Economy. Every sentence does at least one job. Most do two.
