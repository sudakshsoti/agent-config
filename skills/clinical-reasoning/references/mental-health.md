
# 14. Mental Health Assessment Framework

Section 14 of the Clinical Reasoning Framework. Numbering continues from
[`clinical-framework.md`](clinical-framework.md); §1-13 and §15 live there, §16
(longitudinal and chronic care) in [`longitudinal-care.md`](longitudinal-care.md).

## 14.1 When to Apply

Trigger this framework when:
- Patient presents with mood symptoms (low mood, anxiety, irritability, sleep disruption, anhedonia)
- Caregiver/family member reports behavioural changes
- Psychiatric medication is being managed (starts, dose changes, tapers)
- Screening is indicated (postpartum, chronic pain, elderly with new cognitive complaints)
- A somatic presentation has outrun its organic explanation (§14.8)

## 14.2 Screening Tools

**PHQ-9** (depression): Score 0-27. Mild 5-9, Moderate 10-14, Moderately severe 15-19, Severe 20-27.
- Score ≥10: warrants treatment discussion (pharmacotherapy or therapy or both)
- Score ≥15: strong indication for pharmacotherapy
- Question 9 (suicidal ideation) positive at any score: escalate immediately

**GAD-7** (anxiety): Score 0-21. Mild 5-9, Moderate 10-14, Severe 15-21.
- Score ≥10: warrants treatment discussion

**PHQ-2** (ultra-brief depression screen): 2 questions. Score ≥3 → administer full PHQ-9.

**In the perinatal year, EPDS leads.** It excludes the somatic items that pregnancy
and the postpartum confound (sleep, appetite, energy), so a PHQ-9 in a
5-month-postpartum woman reads high for reasons that are not mood. Score both if
you like, but treat EPDS as the tracked instrument and PHQ-9 as corroboration.

## 14.3 Primary Care vs Psychiatry Referral

**Manage in primary care:**
- Mild-moderate depression (PHQ-9 10-14) without suicidality, first episode
- Generalised anxiety without panic attacks or OCD features
- Adjustment disorders with identifiable stressor
- SSRI initiation for straightforward cases

**Refer to psychiatry:**
- Suicidal ideation (active or passive with plan)
- Psychotic features (hallucinations, delusions, paranoia)
- Bipolar features (even suspected: irritability + decreased sleep + grandiosity + pressured speech)
- Treatment-resistant depression (failed 2 adequate SSRI trials — for what "adequate" means, §14.9)
- Complex polypharmacy involving psychiatric medications
- Substance use disorder co-occurring
- Personality disorder features affecting treatment
- Medication taper for psychiatric drugs (coordinate, don't solo)

## 14.4 Indian Psychiatry Access Realities

- Psychiatrist density: ~0.3 per 100,000 population. Long wait times outside metros.
- Metros (Delhi/NCR, Mumbai, Bangalore, Chennai): reasonable access, ₹800-2,500 per consultation
- Tier 2-3 cities: limited availability, telepsychiatry is often the practical option
- Telepsychiatry platforms: Amaha, MindPeers, Practo (psychiatry filter), Tele-MANAS (government, free)
- **Tele-MANAS: 14416 (also 1-800-891-4416), 24×7, 50+ state cells** — the one national number that is actually staffed
- Stigma remains significant: frame recommendations with sensitivity to family dynamics
- Government DMHP (District Mental Health Programme) clinics: free, but overburdened

## 14.6 Pharmacokinetic Engineering (Psychiatric Drugs)

Treat psychiatric dosing as precision engineering, not trial-and-error.

**Half-lives and steady state**: a drug reaches steady state in ~5 half-lives — judge response only after that at an adequate dose. Escitalopram/sertraline ~1 week to steady state; assess response at 4-6 weeks, not days. Fluoxetine is the outlier — parent t½ 1-3 days but active norfluoxetine 7-15 days, so it self-tapers on stopping and washes out slowly (relevant before an MAOI switch). Don't call "non-response" before 4-6 weeks at therapeutic dose.

**CYP450 phenotype** — the engine of inter-individual dosing variance:
- CYP2D6 substrates: paroxetine, fluoxetine, venlafaxine, TCAs, risperidone, aripiprazole, atomoxetine. Poor metabolisers (~1-7%, varies in South Asians) overshoot and toxify; ultra-rapid metabolisers under-respond at standard doses.
- CYP2C19 substrates: escitalopram, citalopram, sertraline, TCAs. PMs run higher levels — FDA caps citalopram at 20mg in CYP2C19 PMs and with strong inhibitors (QT prolongation).
- Inhibitor traps: fluoxetine and paroxetine are strong 2D6 inhibitors that raise their own and co-administered substrate levels (e.g. adding to risperidone → EPS; with tamoxifen → blocks activation, avoid). Fluvoxamine is a strong 1A2/2C19 inhibitor (clozapine levels rocket).

**Titration and switching**: start low to clear the early activation/anxiety/GI bump, then up-titrate to an adequate dose — under-dosing masquerades as treatment failure. Cross-taper when switching (down-titrate the old while up-titrating the new) except dangerous pairs. Never co-administer an MAOI with an SSRI/SNRI/TCA — serotonin syndrome; wash out 2 weeks (5 weeks after fluoxetine) in either direction.

**Therapeutic drug monitoring** — where plasma levels genuinely steer dosing:
- Lithium: 0.6-0.8 mmol/L maintenance, 0.8-1.0 acute mania; 12h trough, recheck 5 days after any dose change; toxic >1.5. Baseline + periodic TSH, creatinine/eGFR, calcium. NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, thiazides and dehydration raise levels.
- Clozapine: 350-600 ng/mL target; mandatory ANC monitoring (agranulocytosis); smoking induces 1A2 (cessation spikes levels); titrate slowly (myocarditis, seizures, ileus).
- Valproate: 50-100 µg/mL; LFTs, ammonia, platelets; teratogen — avoid in women of childbearing potential.

**Augmentation before polypharmacy**: for inadequate SSRI response, optimise the dose first, then *augment rationally* — lithium, low-dose atypical (aripiprazole 2-5mg), or T3 — or switch class (SNRI, mirtazapine, bupropion). Rational stacking with a mechanistic rationale beats blind accumulation.

**Discontinuation kinetics**: short-half-life agents (paroxetine, venlafaxine) cause the worst discontinuation syndrome — taper hyperbolically over months (§14.9, and §6 of the framework for the general taper protocol). Benzodiazepines: always taper, never stop abruptly (seizure risk).

## 14.7 Holistic Formulation (Biopsychosocial-Existential)

A diagnosis is a label; a formulation is an understanding. Build the formulation before reaching for the prescription pad.

**The 4 Ps grid** — populate biological / psychological / social rows against each column:

| | Predisposing | Precipitating | Perpetuating | Protective |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it asks | Why this person is vulnerable | What triggered it now | What keeps it going | What to mobilise |

Biological (genetics, illness, substances), psychological (cognitive style, attachment, trauma), social (work, finances, relationships, housing). The perpetuating column is where treatment usually has the most leverage.

**Measurement-based care**: anchor with PHQ-9/GAD-7 at baseline and each review; titrate to remission (PHQ-9 <5), not merely "better". Track function, sleep and side-effect burden alongside the score. Operational cadence in §14.9.

**The existential layer**: much suffering is not pathology — grief, meaninglessness, moral injury, burnout, a life misaligned with values. Symptoms are signal: ask what the symptom is responding to before deciding it is a disease. Medication can buy the runway — lift the floor enough that a person can do the work — but meaning, relationships and agency are what they fly toward, and pharmacology cannot manufacture them. Name the limits of the prescription honestly.

**When to medicate, when not**: clear indication (moderate-severe, melancholic, psychotic, bipolar, active suicidality, functional collapse) → medicate without hesitation; under-treatment is its own harm. Mild or situational distress with an identifiable cause → therapy, behavioural activation, sleep/exercise/social scaffolding first; don't medicalise a proportionate response to an abnormal situation.

**Indian context**: the family is often the treatment unit — engage caregivers, and work with (not against) stigma. Sleep deprivation, work and financial stress, and joint-family dynamics are frequently the real perpetuating factors; access constraints (§14.4) shape what is actually deliverable.

## 14.8 Diagnostic Discipline Before the Label

A psychiatric diagnosis is a diagnosis of pattern, not of exclusion — but four
checks must clear before the label goes on the chart.

### 14.8.1 Organic Screen

Guideline-backed baseline before labelling a first presentation of mania, psychosis
or treatment-resistant depression (NICE CG185 for mania; Maudsley organic screen
for first-episode psychosis):

CBC, U&E + calcium, LFT, RFT, TFT (TSH + free T4), HbA1c or fasting glucose, fasting
lipids, B12 + folate, ECG for QTc, urine drug screen, pregnancy test where relevant.
Add vitamin D where fatigue or bone symptoms dominate; HIV/VDRL where risk or
psychosis; structural neuroimaging on red flags (focal signs, late first onset,
atypical course) — not by habit.

India-specific prior probabilities make three of these high-yield rather than
ritual: B12 deficiency runs 47-77% in vegetarians, NFHS-5 anaemia ~57% in
non-pregnant women 15-49, hypothyroidism ~10-12% in women.

**Sleep apnoea**: STOP-BANG (Snoring, Tired, Observed apnoea, Pressure, BMI >35,
Age >50, Neck >40cm, male). ≥3 moderate risk, ≥5 high. Untreated OSA presents as
treatment-resistant depression and as cognitive complaint.

**Iatrogenic sweep** — always ask what else they are on: corticosteroids,
isotretinoin, levetiracetam, montelukast, efavirenz, interferon, beta-blockers,
varenicline, high-dose opioids, anticholinergic load (§10.1).

### 14.8.2 Delirium First in the Elderly

New confusion, fluctuation or acute cognitive change in an older or polypharmacy
patient is delirium until proven otherwise — missing it is the highest-cost error
in this section (mortality, prolonged admission, institutionalisation).

- **4AT** (alertness, AMT4, attention/months-backwards, acute change; 0-12): ≥4 possible delirium, 1-3 cognitive impairment without delirium, 0 unlikely. No training required, ~2 minutes.
- **CAM** requires acute fluctuating course **and** inattention **plus** disorganised thinking or altered consciousness. Rater training needed — 4AT is the clinic tool.
- Depression in the elderly mimics dementia (pseudodementia): GDS-15 alongside HMSE, and re-test cognition after mood treatment.

### 14.8.3 The Bipolar Gate

Screen for bipolarity before writing any antidepressant. Treatment-emergent
affective switch is the avoidable harm.

MDQ ≥7 has ~95% specificity but poor sensitivity in primary care and misses
bipolar II; HCL-32 ≥14 is more sensitive but inflates with anxiety and substance
comorbidity. Neither is a gate on its own. The six clinical features outperform
both questionnaires:

1. Onset before 25
2. Episodicity rather than chronic low mood
3. Atypical features — hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis
4. First-degree family history of bipolar disorder
5. Postpartum onset
6. Antidepressant non-response, rapid response, or activation/switch on previous trials

Count episodicity properly: a hypomanic window needs ≥4 consecutive days of
persistently elevated or irritable mood with changed function, mania ≥7 days (or
any duration with hospitalisation or psychosis). A single bad night, or
goal-directed overwork without mood change, does not count. Where the history is
collateral and uncorroborated, count it as present but say so.

Two or more present → treat as suspected bipolar spectrum: do not start an
antidepressant as monotherapy; refer (§14.3).

**While awaiting the psychiatrist** — the gap this rule creates is real, especially
for a tele-only or perinatal patient who cannot be handed a mood stabiliser today.
Hold with: sleep protection as the first intervention (a protected 5-6 hour block,
shared night feeds, fixed wake time — sleep loss is the commonest switch trigger),
behavioural activation, alcohol and stimulant removal, a named family observer for
switch signs (reduced sleep need with increased energy, pressured speech,
expansive plans, spending), and a short review interval measured in days. Do not
bridge with a benzodiazepine by reflex; if sedation is genuinely needed the
decision belongs with the psychiatrist who will also own the mood stabiliser.

### 14.8.4 Somatic Idiom and Cultural Formulation

Roughly a third of Indian primary-care attenders present distress somatically:
weakness, "gas", burning feet, palpitations, non-specific pain. Dhat syndrome
(preoccupation with semen loss plus fatigue, weakness, low-back pain, anxiety)
is the prototype culture-bound presentation — DSM-5 dropped the separate code and
treats it as a cultural concept of distress; Indian practice still recognises the
presentation. It is an explanatory model, not a diagnosis.

Rule: rule out the organic cause properly, then quantify with PHQ-15 or SSS-8 and
explore the explanatory model — never dismiss the body complaint to get to the
mind one. The patient's physical account is the true account of their experience.

**DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview**, four domains, 16 questions:
1. Cultural definition of the problem (Q1-3) — what they call it, what troubles most
2. Cause, context, support (Q4-7) — what caused it, who helps, who makes it worse, identity
3. Self-coping and past help-seeking (Q8-13) — including folk, religious and faith-healer pathways, and barriers
4. Current help-seeking (Q14-16) — expectations of this consultation, preferences, concerns about the clinician relationship

Domain 3 is the one that changes management in India: faith-healer and Ayurvedic
pathways are usually concurrent, not prior, and the family's model determines
adherence more than the prescription does.

### 14.8.5 Mental State Examination

Carry all ten domains; generic reasoning drops attitude and motor activity or
collapses mood into thought content.

Appearance and behaviour · attitude · motor activity · speech (rate, volume,
articulation, coherence) · mood and affect (subjective and observed, congruence,
range, reactivity) · thought process (form) · thought content · perception ·
cognition (orientation, attention, memory) · insight and judgement.

### 14.8.6 Diagnostic Time-Out

Before committing to a primary diagnosis, state three answers explicitly — cognitive
factors contribute to ~74% of diagnostic errors and premature closure is the
commonest one:

1. What else could this be? (name at least two alternatives)
2. What must not be missed? (the lethal or reversible one)
3. What does not fit? (the finding the leading diagnosis fails to explain)

If the answer to (3) is "nothing", the differential was probably anchored rather
than reasoned.

## 14.9 Longitudinal Psychiatric Care

The first prescription is the easy part. This section is what a review visit does.

### 14.9.1 Measurement-Based Care

Rescore at every contact, minimum every 4 weeks in the acute phase. MBC roughly
halves time to response (median 5.6 vs 11.6 weeks, Guo 2015) — the effect comes
from acting on the number, not from collecting it.

- **Response**: ≥50% reduction in PHQ-9 (or GAD-7) from baseline
- **Remission**: PHQ-9 <5, GAD-7 <5 — this is the target, not "better"
- **Function alongside symptoms**: WSAS (0-40) or WHODAS-12 (0-48). Symptom remission with WSAS >20 is not a treated patient.

**The week-4 rule**: <20% reduction at 4 weeks on a therapeutic dose predicts
non-response. Act then — optimise the dose — rather than waiting out week 8 on a
dose that was never adequate.

### 14.9.2 Sequencing and Treatment Resistance

Order of operations after an inadequate response: confirm adherence → optimise
dose to the top of the tolerated range → switch class or augment → then refer.
Augmentation with best evidence: aripiprazole 2-5mg (start 1-2mg, max 10mg),
lithium, or T3. Switch options: SNRI, mirtazapine, bupropion.

**Adequate trial** = therapeutic dose, 6-8 weeks, adherence confirmed. Two failed
adequate trials in one episode = treatment-resistant depression (FDA/EMA
convention). Staging taxonomies beyond that add classification, not decisions.

**Calibrate expectations honestly.** STAR\*D level 1 remission was 28-33% and level
4 about 13%; the frequently quoted 67% cumulative figure does not survive the 2023
BMJ Open reanalysis, which puts protocol-stipulated cumulative remission at 35%.
Tell the patient the real number — it makes step 2 a plan rather than a failure.

### 14.9.3 Maintenance and Relapse Prevention

- **First episode**: continue the effective dose ≥6-9 months after remission. Dose reduction "because they're well" is the commonest cause of relapse.
- **Recurrent** (≥2-3 episodes), severe, chronic, psychotic, or residual symptoms: 2 years to indefinite. Continuation cuts relapse odds by roughly 70% (Geddes 2003).
- **Bipolar**: lithium is the maintenance standard; prophylaxis is the indication, levels the discipline (§14.10.3).
- **Antipsychotics**: maintenance is the default. The discontinuation question is genuinely unresolved — RADAR (2023) found more relapse and no social-function gain in the taper arm, while Wunderink's 7-year follow-up found better long-term functioning after early dose reduction. Present it as a trade-off with explicit consent, not a recommendation.

**Relapse signature**: write down, with the patient, the earliest 3-4 changes that
preceded their last episode — usually sleep, withdrawal, irritability, or a
specific thought pattern — plus what to do and whom to call at each. Review it at
every visit; a stale crisis plan is not a crisis plan.

### 14.9.4 The Psychiatric Review Visit

Run this checklist rather than "how are you feeling?":

1. PHQ-9 / GAD-7 with delta from baseline and last visit; WSAS or WHODAS-12
2. Adherence — normalised probe (§16.4) — and cost as an adherence factor
3. Side-effect burden, including sexual side effects and weight, asked directly
4. Sleep, alcohol and substance use, caffeine
5. Medical comorbidity and the monitoring calendar due dates (§14.10)
6. Suicidality and, where relevant, risk to others
7. Function — work, relationships, self-care — in the patient's own terms
8. Relapse signature vs current state; crisis plan currency
9. Decision point: continue, optimise, switch, augment, or begin taper — and the date of the next review

### 14.9.5 Stopping: Hyperbolic Taper

Receptor occupancy is hyperbolic in dose, so equal milligram steps are not equal
effect steps — the last few milligrams do the most work (Horowitz & Taylor 2019;
Maudsley Deprescribing Guidelines 2024; RCPsych 2024 position on withdrawal).

- Reduce by **10-25% of the current dose** per step, not of the original dose
- Slow further at the bottom of the range; liquid or compounded doses are often needed below the smallest tablet
- Pace by symptoms, patient-led, over months rather than weeks
- Paroxetine and venlafaxine are the shortest leash; fluoxetine is the most forgiving (self-tapering)
- Benzodiazepines: taper always, never abrupt (seizure risk)

**Withdrawal vs relapse** — the discrimination that decides the next step:

| | Withdrawal | Relapse |
|---|---|---|
| Onset after change | Days to 2 weeks | Weeks to months |
| Character | Dizziness, "brain zaps", nausea, insomnia, irritability, flu-like | Return of the original depressive or anxious syndrome |
| Course | Fluctuates, often improves within weeks | Progressive |
| Response to reinstatement | Relief within days | No rapid relief |

Prepare the patient before the first reduction: naming withdrawal in advance
prevents it being read as proof the illness is back.

## 14.10 Psychotropic Monitoring Calendars

A drug started without its calendar is a drug started badly. State the next due
date on the prescription.

### 14.10.1 Antipsychotics — Metabolic

Baseline → 12 weeks → at least annually (NICE CG178, surveillance-confirmed 2025);
the ADA/APA grid adds 4 and 8 weeks in the high-risk or rapid-gain patient.

| Parameter | Baseline | 4wk | 8wk | 12wk | Quarterly | Annual |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weight, BMI, waist | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Blood pressure | ✓ | | | ✓ | | ✓ |
| Fasting glucose / HbA1c | ✓ | | | ✓ | | ✓ |
| Fasting lipids | ✓ | | | ✓ | | ✓ |
| Prolactin | ✓ | | | ✓ (3mo) | | ✓ / if symptomatic |
| ECG (QTc) | ✓ | | | on dose change or added QT drug | | ✓ if risk |
| AIMS (tardive dyskinesia) | ✓ | | | | 6-monthly if high risk | 12-monthly if low risk |

Any dose increase or added agent restarts the 12-week clock. Weight gain >5% of
body weight is an action point, not an observation: switch consideration,
metformin, or dietetic referral.

**Prolactin**: highest with risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, haloperidol;
modest with olanzapine; spared by quetiapine and clozapine; lowered by
aripiprazole. Ask about galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, gynaecomastia, sexual
dysfunction — patients do not volunteer these.

**QTc ladder**: normal <440ms men, <470ms women. 440/470-499 → reduce dose or
switch to a lower-risk agent, correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺, repeat ECG. ≥500ms → stop the
offending drug, cardiology input. **Δ ≥60ms from baseline acts even below 500ms.**
Highest-risk agents: IV haloperidol, ziprasidone, thioridazine, pimozide; also
citalopram and escitalopram at dose.

### 14.10.2 Clozapine

- **ANC**: weekly × 18 weeks → fortnightly to week 52 → monthly thereafter, and for 4 weeks after stopping
- **Myocarditis** peaks weeks 1-4: hs-troponin + CRP at baseline and days 7, 14, 21, 28, with vitals and symptom review. Stop for troponin >2× ULN or CRP >100 mg/L with symptoms
- **Constipation**: prophylactic laxative from the start and bowel review at every early contact — ileus kills more clozapine patients than agranulocytosis does
- **Seizures**: dose-related; risk climbs above 600mg/day or levels >600 ng/mL
- **Smoking**: CYP1A2 induction; cessation raises levels 50-70% within days — pre-emptively reduce 30-50% and check a level. This includes an admission where the patient simply cannot smoke.

### 14.10.3 Lithium

- Target 0.6-0.8 mmol/L maintenance; 0.8-1.0 for acute mania or relapse-prone patients with a documented rationale
- **12h trough** (10-14h acceptable); level 5-7 days after start or any change, weekly until stable, 3-monthly through year 1, 6-monthly when stable
- Stay 3-monthly indefinitely if age ≥65, abnormal eGFR/TFT/calcium, an interacting drug, or a level ≥0.8
- U&E/eGFR, TFT, calcium, weight 6-monthly (U&E 2-3 monthly in the first 6 months)
- Toxicity: >1.2 caution, >1.5 toxic (vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion), >2.5 dialysis territory
- Precipitants to counsel every time: NSAIDs, thiazides, ACEi/ARB, dehydration, low-salt diet, febrile illness, vomiting/diarrhoea — all of which are routine in an Indian summer
- Avoid initiation if eGFR <30; in CKD, 3-monthly lithium and eGFR together

### 14.10.4 Valproate

Absolute contraindication in pregnancy: major malformation risk 6-10%,
neurodevelopmental disorder 30-40%. Never start in a woman of childbearing
potential without a documented contraception plan and a discussion recorded in the
notes; prefer an alternative outright.

The UK MHRA's January 2024 rule — no new patient under 55 of either sex without
two specialists independently documenting that no alternative exists — is **UK
regulation, not Indian law**. CDSCO requires the pregnancy contraindication and
warning labelling, with no two-specialist requirement. Apply the MHRA standard as
good practice if you choose; do not represent it as an Indian legal obligation.

Monitoring: LFT, FBC/platelets at baseline and periodically; ammonia if
encephalopathic features; level 50-100 µg/mL where adherence or toxicity is in
question.

### 14.10.5 Antidepressant Safety

- **Hyponatraemia/SIADH**: elderly, diuretics, low baseline sodium. Onset days to 2 weeks. Check Na⁺ at baseline and within 2 weeks in at-risk patients; stop or switch if <130 or symptomatic
- **Bleeding**: SSRI/SNRI plus NSAID, antiplatelet or anticoagulant is additive GI bleed risk — add a PPI (pantoprazole; §4.3)
- **Dose caps (MHRA)**: citalopram 40mg, 20mg if >65 or hepatic impairment or CYP2C19 poor metaboliser; escitalopram 20mg, 10mg in the same groups
- **Serotonin toxicity — Hunter criteria**: serotonergic agent plus one of spontaneous clonus; inducible clonus with agitation or diaphoresis; ocular clonus with agitation or diaphoresis; tremor with hyperreflexia; hypertonia with temperature >38°C and ocular or inducible clonus. **Clonus is the discriminator** — Hunter supersedes Sternbach
- **Under-25s**: the boxed warning stands; monitor weekly to fortnightly early in treatment. Do not withhold indicated treatment on the strength of the warning

### 14.10.6 Pregnancy and Lactation

- **SSRIs**: no major teratogenic signal; small absolute increases (cardiac ~+0.3-0.5%, PPHN ~3/1000 with late exposure, transient neonatal adaptation). Sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram preferred; avoid paroxetine if alternatives exist
- **Lithium**: Ebstein anomaly ~1/1500 (0.07%) against ~1/20,000 background — **not** the 1/400 figure that older texts still carry; any cardiac malformation ~1.9-2.4% vs ~1.2-2.1% baseline, dose-dependent. Monthly levels, careful peripartum fluid management, neonatal watch
- **Valproate**: absolute contraindication (above)
- **Lactation (relative infant dose)**: sertraline ~1% and olanzapine/quetiapine <0.1% are the preferred choices; escitalopram and citalopram compatible; lithium RID high with infant serum ~1/3 of maternal — avoid, or co-manage with paediatrics and monitor infant lithium, TFT and renal function. Run every psychotropic through the LactMed layer (§5)
- **Sedatives in lactation**: melatonin is not established in breastfeeding — endogenous in breast milk, no adequate RID data; time a dose immediately after the last evening feed if used, and say the uncertainty out loud rather than implying safety. Benzodiazepines transfer and accumulate in the neonate (sedation, poor feeding) — single doses tolerable, regular dosing is not; prefer the shortest-acting agent, watch feeding and tone, and avoid entirely in a preterm or jaundiced infant. Z-drugs add nothing over a benzodiazepine here and are barred from teleconsult anyway (§14.11.1)
- **Antipsychotics in lactation**: olanzapine and quetiapine are the best-documented (RID <0.1%); monitor the infant for sedation, feeding difficulty and extrapyramidal signs, and check maternal metabolic parameters on the standard calendar (§14.10.1). Clozapine is contraindicated in breastfeeding (infant agranulocytosis risk)

## 14.11 Indian Regulatory Constraints on Psychiatric Prescribing

### 14.11.1 Teleconsultation Prescribing

Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 (BoG in supersession of MCI, 25 March 2020,
Appendix 5 to the IMC Ethics Regulations) with the psychiatry-specific NIMHANS/IPS/TSI
Telepsychiatry Operational Guidelines 2020. Four categories:

- **List O** — OTC: paracetamol, ORS, antacids, vitamins. Any mode.
- **List A** — first consult permitted by **live video only**; any mode (text/audio/video) for tele-follow-up. Psychiatry List A per the MCI amendment of 11 April 2020: imipramine, escitalopram, fluoxetine; lithium carbonate, carbamazepine, sodium valproate; haloperidol, risperidone, olanzapine; trihexyphenidyl; phenobarbitone, phenytoin; **clonazepam, clobazam**. Injectables (fluphenazine, haloperidol, promethazine) only via collaborative tele-video consult with an RMP or health worker present.
- **List B** — tele-follow-up only (same psychiatrist, within 6 months of an in-person consult): sertraline, paroxetine, mirtazapine, bupropion, duloxetine, venlafaxine and other antidepressants; aripiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine, amisulpride and other antipsychotics; lamotrigine, oxcarbazepine, divalproex; donepezil, memantine; atomoxetine, clonidine, modafinil; buspirone, pregabalin, propranolol; anti-craving agents (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram, topiramate, baclofen).
- **List C — prohibited online**: anything in Schedule X of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, and any substance under the NDPS Act 1985. The guidelines name **zolpidem and lorazepam explicitly** — both are on the DMHP essential list but fall under NDPS, so they cannot be prescribed online at all.

Note the tension inside the source: clonazepam and clobazam appear in psychiatry's
List A while §7.4 prohibits benzodiazepines generically as NDPS substances. The
safe reading, and the one to state: benzodiazepines other than clonazepam/clobazam
are prohibited online; clonazepam/clobazam only on a live video consult with the
NDPS/Schedule H1 obligations documented; zolpidem and lorazepam never.

Other hard rules: first psychiatric teleconsult must be **video**, not text or
audio; asynchronous consultation (a health worker records, the psychiatrist
prescribes later) is prohibited; prescribe by generic name; document the patient's
physical location, because emergency routing and mandatory reporting follow the
patient's jurisdiction; issue a prescription only after at least a provisional
diagnosis; lithium still needs its baseline RFT, TFT and electrolytes before the
first dose, teleconsult or not.

### 14.11.2 Schedule H1, Schedule X, NDPS

**Schedule H1** (D&C Rules, GSR 588(E), 30 August 2013) covers alprazolam,
clonazepam, zolpidem, buprenorphine and specified antibiotics/anti-TB drugs. The
pharmacy must keep a separate H1 register — prescriber name, address and
registration number, patient name and address, drug, batch, expiry, quantity —
retained **three years**, and the pack carries the "Schedule H1 Drug — Warning"
box. Write the registration number legibly; an H1 prescription without it is the
pharmacist's problem and then yours.

**Schedule X** and NDPS substances need separate licensing, stricter storage and
their own registers; NDPS prescriptions follow the narcotic prescription rules
(letterhead, dose, duration, patient particulars).

### 14.11.3 Mental Healthcare Act 2017 — Treatment-Relevant Provisions

- **§5 advance directive**: an adult may state in advance how they wish to be treated, and which treatments they refuse. Check for one before treating a patient who lacks capacity now.
- **§14 nominated representative**: the person who consents where capacity is absent, and for a minor. Not automatically the nearest relative.
- **§23 confidentiality**: consider writing the ICD/DSM code rather than a narrative diagnosis on a prescription that will pass through family hands.
- **§25 basic medical records**: the patient has a right of access; maintain records to that standard, teleconsult included.
- **§95**: unmodified ECT is prohibited outright; ECT in a minor requires the guardian's consent **and** prior State Mental Health Authority permission.
- **§106**: do not prescribe or certify outside your field.

## 14.12 Anxiety Disorders — Panic, Agoraphobia and the Depression Mislabel

### 14.12.1 Suspecting an Anxiety Disorder Behind a Depression Label

A depression that will not remit is not automatically a pharmacology problem. Five
triggers should prompt a diagnostic re-look rather than another switch or
augmentation:

- A chronic somatic symptom (palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness, GI upset)
  that never remits across adequate antidepressant trials
- The anxiety symptom predates the mood symptom in the timeline, not the reverse
- Body-first presentation with no cognitive content the patient can name — asked
  what the thought is during a bad episode, the answer is "no thoughts", not a
  catastrophic belief
- A situational rather than diurnal pattern — worse in specific places or
  circumstances, not worse at a particular time of day
- Symptom worse in unstructured time (weekends, holidays) and better when work or
  routine imposes structure and distraction

**A depression that has not remitted across two adequate trials (§14.9.2) should
have the diagnosis re-examined before the drug is changed again** — augmenting or
switching for the third time treats the label, not the patient, if the label was
wrong.

### 14.12.2 Panic Attack and Panic Disorder

A **panic attack** is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort peaking within
minutes, with four or more of: palpitations/pounding heart; sweating; trembling;
shortness of breath or smothering; choking sensation; chest pain; nausea or
abdominal distress; dizziness/unsteadiness/faintness; chills or heat sensations;
paraesthesias; derealisation or depersonalisation; fear of losing control or
going crazy; fear of dying. An **expected** attack has an obvious situational cue
(a phobic trigger); an **unexpected** attack has none — it is the unexpected ones
that anchor a panic disorder diagnosis.

**Panic disorder** = recurrent unexpected panic attacks, **plus** one month or
more of persistent worry about further attacks or their consequences, or a
significant maladaptive change in behaviour because of them (the avoidance).
The attack alone is not the disorder; the disorder is the attack plus what the
patient now does to prevent the next one.

Do not confuse a discrete panic attack with the chronic low-grade somatic
anxiety it is routinely mistaken for on both sides of the desk: a patient
describing "always tense, always on edge" with a background hum of palpitations
and forgetfulness is not thereby having panic attacks — ask for the discrete
peaks separately, and expect them to be rare ("once or twice, years ago") even
in an agoraphobia that has been active for over a decade. The peaks establish
the diagnosis; their rarity does not disprove it.

### 14.12.3 Agoraphobia

Fear or avoidance in **two or more** of five situation clusters, where the fear
concerns escape being difficult or help being unavailable if panic-like or
incapacitating symptoms occur, present for **six months or more**, with the
situations avoided, endured with intense dread, or requiring a companion:

| Situation cluster | Example | Endorsed? |
|---|---|---|
| Public transport | Bus, train, flight, cab alone | |
| Open spaces | Car parks, marketplaces, bridges, long drives | |
| Enclosed spaces | Shops, cinema halls, lifts, queues that cannot be left | |
| Crowds or queues | Malls, weddings, waiting lines | |
| Outside the home alone | Any errand, commute, or full day away from home unaccompanied | |

This is a five-minute table, not a specialist instrument — the whole point is
that it is quick to run and in an undiagnosed case nobody ran it. Two or more
endorsed at six months or longer is agoraphobia; screen it in the same visit as
the panic-attack history, because the two are asked about together or neither
gets asked about at all.

### 14.12.4 The Panic-to-Avoidance Sequence

The developmental model that explains why this becomes a chronic, unremitting
condition rather than a self-limiting one:

**index panic attack → anticipatory anxiety about recurrence → avoidance of
situations where an attack would be intolerable or inescapable → avoidance
relieves the anticipatory distress and is therefore reinforced → the avoidance
becomes the disorder and outlives the attacks that started it.**

A patient can present a decade or more after the index attack with the attacks
themselves nearly extinct ("once or twice, years ago") while the avoidance
built around them has only grown — more situations added, more of life
rearranged. **Once avoidance is the maintaining mechanism, no dose of any drug
treats it**; the drug can quiet the physiology while the avoidance keeps the
disorder alive underneath.

### 14.12.5 Safety Behaviours

A safety behaviour is anything the patient does to prevent a feared outcome
that never disconfirms the fear because the outcome was never going to happen.
Common ones: the PRN benzodiazepine kept "just in case"; the always-present
companion; the seat near the exit; the carried water bottle; repeated phone
checks; a mentally mapped route home; an always-available escape car or driver.

Each one preserves the belief that survival was conditional on the behaviour —
"I only got through it because I had the tablet/my wife/the exit seat" — which
blocks exactly the disconfirming learning that exposure depends on. **A PRN
benzodiazepine prescribed for agoraphobic anxiety is not a neutral bridge; it is
an active maintaining factor**, reinforced every time it is taken in response to
symptoms rather than on a schedule.

For a patient already benzodiazepine-dependent from PRN use (five or more
consecutive days is enough to raise rebound risk on stopping), abrupt
withdrawal is unsafe and untimed tapering mid-crisis is counterproductive. The
practical resolution: **convert PRN dosing to a fixed low dose at set clock
times**, decoupling the tablet from the symptom rather than letting fear
trigger the dose; log every dose taken; taper only later, under a prescriber,
never mid-exposure and never abruptly after consecutive daily use.

### 14.12.6 Treatment

**Pharmacotherapy, first-line**: SSRI — sertraline (Daxid, Serlift) or
escitalopram (Nexito, Stalopam) are the usual Indian choices (§14.5).
Venlafaxine XR (Venlor XR) has good evidence specifically in panic disorder and
is a reasonable first-line alternative, not a fallback.

**Panic patients are exquisitely sensitive to initial activation. Start at half
the usual starting dose or lower, and warn the patient in advance that early
jitteriness, if it occurs, is expected and transient.** An unwarned activation
surge in a panic-prone patient reads to them as proof the illness is worsening,
and is a common, avoidable cause of abandoning a drug that would otherwise have
worked.

Medication reduces attack frequency and anticipatory anxiety. It does **not**
treat avoidance. Exposure-based CBT — graded in-vivo exposure to avoided
situations, interoceptive exposure to the feared bodily sensations themselves,
and systematic dropping of safety behaviours (§14.12.5) — is a required
treatment component for agoraphobia, not an adjunct to consider later.
**Combined pharmacotherapy plus exposure-based CBT beats either alone for
agoraphobia specifically.**

Benzodiazepines: short-term use only. There is evidence they impair the
extinction learning that exposure work relies on — a patient doing graded
exposure while on a standing (not just occasional) benzodiazepine may be doing
the exercises without being able to bank the learning.

### 14.12.7 Screening Blind Spot

**GAD-7 and PHQ-9 do not detect panic disorder or agoraphobia.** GAD-7 asks
about worry, not about avoidance or discrete panic peaks; an agoraphobic
patient whose life has already been rearranged around avoidance can score low
precisely because the avoidance is working and the situations that would
provoke anxiety are no longer being entered.

The single question that catches it, and the one to add as a standing item in
any psychiatric review where a somatic symptom has not remitted:

**"Are there places or situations you avoid, or can only face if someone comes
with you?"**

Once identified, the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS) is the instrument for
tracking severity and response, in the same role PHQ-9/GAD-7 play for
depression and generalised anxiety (§14.9.1).

### 14.12.8 Indian Context

Exposure-based CBT is available in most metros (private, typically ₹1,500-4,000
per session, 12-20 sessions for a full agoraphobia protocol) but scarce outside
them; tier 2-3 access mirrors the general psychiatry shortfall (§14.4) and is
often worse, since exposure therapy needs a trained CBT therapist, not just a
psychiatrist. The practical reality is that most Indian psychiatric care for
this presentation is pharmacotherapy-only by default — a referral for
exposure-based work has to be made explicitly, by name, or it does not happen;
"therapy would help" without naming the modality and the referral path is not a
referral (§14.13).

Teleprescribing constraints (§14.11.1) apply as for any psychiatric drug here:
SSRIs and venlafaxine are List A/List B depending on the specific molecule;
clonazepam is List A but restricted to a live video consult with Schedule H1
obligations documented; propranolol is List B, tele-follow-up only.

### 14.12.9 The Misdiagnosis Pattern

The pattern that lets this run for a decade or more is consistent enough to
name in general terms: a somatic anxiety symptom emerging in adolescence or
early adulthood; sequential antidepressants escalated to ceiling doses across
years, each one a reasonable choice in isolation; each subsequent clinician
inheriting the prior diagnosis and prescription rather than re-taking the
history from the index event forward; and avoidance never asked about, because
no instrument in routine use asks about it (§14.12.7).

**When a symptom has survived every adequate trial, re-take the history rather
than escalate the dose — and ask specifically about avoidance.**
