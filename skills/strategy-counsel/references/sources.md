# Sources — Kautilya and Machiavelli at working depth

The two traditions this skill reasons from. Consult when a recommendation turns on a
structural claim — adjacency, posture, timing, what creates permanent enemies — rather
than on the specifics of the situation. Cite the concept, not the citation. Nobody needs a
bibliography mid-answer.

## Textual note

**Arthashastra** — Kautilya (Chanakya, Vishnugupta). Traditionally c. 300 BCE, compiled
and redacted over centuries, likely reaching final form ~150–300 CE. Kangle's edition is
the standard. Fifteen books, mostly administrative; the strategic material sits in Books
6–14. Distinct from **Chanakya Niti**, a later aphoristic compilation — popular, thinner,
not the source here.

**Machiavelli** — *The Prince* (1513) is about acquiring and holding principalities.
*Discourses on Livy* (1517) is the larger and more useful book for anyone operating inside
an institution without formal authority. Where the two differ, the Discourses usually
applies better to a workplace. Reading only The Prince is the common error.

## Kautilya — structures

**Rajamandala (circle of states).** Alignment follows adjacency, not affinity. The party
whose scope borders yours is the structural rival; the party beyond them is the structural
ally. Neither requires anyone to dislike anyone. Two other positions matter: the
**madhyama**, the middle power who can tip either way and is therefore courted by both,
and the **udasina**, the indifferent party stronger than everyone, whose entry ends the
contest rather than resolving it. Escalating to an udasina is not winning — it is
dissolving the game and forfeiting the standing you would have gained by settling it
yourself.

**Saptanga (seven constituents).** Swami (the ruler), amatya (ministers), janapada
(territory and people), durga (fortifications), kosha (treasury), danda (force), mitra
(allies). The point is inventory: power is not one quantity. **Vyasana** is the calamity
afflicting each limb, and the text ranks which weakness is worse than which. Kautilya's
own precedence: the treasury first — all undertakings depend on finance.

**Kshaya, vyaya, labha.** Every undertaking assessed on three terms: loss, expenditure,
gain. Cost and depletion are separate questions. A move can be affordable and still leave
you unable to make the next one.

**Shadgunya (six measures).** Postures, not a ladder — held simultaneously toward
different parties. Sandhi (accord), vigraha (hostility), asana (holding position), yana
(preparing to move), samshraya (seeking shelter with a stronger power), dvaidhibhava
(accord with one while opposing another). Samshraya is classically understood as
cost-bearing: the sheltered party accepts subordination as the price.

**Upayas, extended.** Sama, dana, bheda, danda — plus, in Kamandaka's *Nitisara*,
**upeksha** (deliberate overlooking) and **maya** (engineered appearance). Upeksha is a
named instrument, not an absence of strategy. Maya is the plain name for most of the gray
register: selective framing, letting a favourable assumption stand, controlling venue and
timing.

**Gudhapurusha.** The covert apparatus, and the reason Kautilya's sequence is gather,
deliberate, act — in that order. Analysis that begins from your own account of events is
analysis of your account.

**Upadha.** Four secret tests of a minister's loyalty before entrusting him with anything.
The usable form is not deception: test an ally cheaply before betting on them. Give a small
piece of information and observe where it travels; ask the minor favour before the major
one.

**Prakriti-kopa.** Disaffection among constituents, sorted into the enraged, the
frightened, the greedy, and the proud — each handled differently. A counterparty resisting
out of fear needs reassurance; one resisting out of pride needs a face-saving exit; one
resisting out of greed needs a trade. Diagnosing the wrong type is why the right tactic
fails.

**Danda-niti, proportion.** Punishment too severe provokes revolt; too lax invites
**matsyanyaya**, the law of the fishes, where the big eat the small. Force is calibrated
or it is counterproductive.

## Machiavelli — dynamics

**Virtù and fortuna** (*Prince* 25). Fortune arbitrates half our actions. The remedy is the
dikes and dams built in calm weather — alliances made before they are needed, which is the
only time they are cheap.

**Riscontro** (*Prince* 25; *Discourses* III.9). Success is the match between one's mode of
proceeding and the times; failure is the inability to change one's nature when the times
change. The cautious man prospers in cautious times and is ruined when the moment demands
impetuosity. This is Machiavelli's actual theory of failure, and it applies to any fixed
default mode.

**Injuries and benefits** (*Prince* 8). Harms delivered in a single stroke are tasted less
and resented briefly; benefits distributed slowly are savoured longer. Cruelty "badly used"
is that which grows over time rather than diminishing. Sequencing, not severity, is the
variable.

**Hatred** (*Prince* 17, 19). Be feared if you must, but never hated — and hatred comes
chiefly from touching men's property and their honour. "Men forget the death of their
father sooner than the loss of their patrimony." The office equivalents are credit and
scope.

**Auxiliaries and mercenaries** (*Prince* 12–13). Borrowed arms are most dangerous when
they win, because the victory belongs to the lender. A prince who conquers with
auxiliaries emerges weaker in standing than he entered.

**Fox and lion** (*Prince* 18). Snares and wolves require different faculties. Paired with
*Prince* 15 — attend to "the effectual truth of the thing rather than the imagination of
it", which is the methodological commitment this skill inherits: describe what happens,
not what ought to.

**Against neutrality** (*Prince* 21). The prince who does not declare is despised by the
loser and unrespected by the winner. Directly contradicts asana. See the tension table
below.

**Productive conflict** (*Discourses* I.4). The tumults between plebs and senate made Rome
free. Conflict inside an institution is generative, not pathological — a counterweight to
treating every friction as a problem to be resolved.

**Accusation vs calumny** (*Discourses* I.7–8). Open, on-the-record challenge strengthens
an institution; private backchannel complaint corrodes it and rebounds on the complainant.

**Conspiracies** (*Discourses* III.6, the longest chapter in the corpus). They fail
overwhelmingly in the disclosure phase, and risk scales with the number of confidants. A
direct constraint on alliance-building: every additional person recruited multiplies
exposure.

## Where the two disagree — adjudicate, do not average

| Question | Kautilya | Machiavelli | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waiting | Asana and upeksha are legitimate postures, often correct | Neutrality is the worst option (21) | Is the contest already decided between two parties who will both remember? If yes, Machiavelli. If the outcome is open and time improves your position, Kautilya. |
| Force | Calibrated, proportionate, institutionalised (danda-niti) | Concentrated, decisive, done at once (8) | Repeated game with an institution watching → Kautilya. One-shot, or the harm is unavoidable → Machiavelli. |
| Allies | Build the mandala; alliance is structural | Auxiliaries weaken you; conspiracies leak (12–13, III.6) | Alliance by shared adjacency is cheap and durable. Alliance requiring coordination and secrecy is expensive and leaks. |
| Deception | Maya is a named instrument among others | Appearance matters more than possession (18) | Kautilya treats it as one tool with costs; Machiavelli as a general condition. Prefer Kautilya's framing — it keeps the gray register bounded. |
| Advisors | The tested minister (upadha) | The prince must invite truth from a chosen few, or drown in flattery (23) | Both point the same way, and both apply to this skill's own conduct. |
