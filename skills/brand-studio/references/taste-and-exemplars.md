# Taste and exemplars

The taste layer. This is what to pattern-match against instead of a generic average of "good
branding". It has two parts: a small set of **method exemplars** that hold for any brand (the
moves the best brands made, and why), and a per-brand **anchor set** assembled for the brand in
play. The method exemplars are stable. Rebuild the anchor set each engagement.

**How to read a reference.** Never "they look nice". Always: the **belief** they sell on (not
the product, the belief), **where the spec sits** (do they lead with material and proof, or with
belief and let proof support it), how they handle the **founder story** (founder-led, belief-led,
or faceless), and the **one thing to steal, one thing to avoid.**

## Direction versus ceiling

Two kinds of reference, and confusing them is the common mistake.

- **Direction references** are brands in or near this category the brand could credibly move
  toward. They set the actual target.
- **Ceiling references** are brands operating at a level of craft or budget the brand cannot
  match yet. They set the taste bar, not the plan. Admire the execution; do not copy the moves
  that only a ceiling-brand budget makes possible.

When citing a reference, say which kind it is. A direction reference earns a "do this"; a
ceiling reference earns an "aim here, but not yet, and not like this".

## Method exemplars

Stable across categories because they are about mechanics, not aesthetics. Each is the move,
the proof it happened, and the transferable lesson.

- **Apple — belief over spec.** Sold "1,000 songs in your pocket" and left "5GB" underneath as
  evidence. The spec never disappears; it stops being the headline. Lead with the outcome the
  customer feels, demote the number to proof.
- **Warby Parker — name the villain.** Sold "eyewear is artificially expensive and access is a
  right", with "$95" as proof. A belief with an antagonist travels further than a feature list.
- **Patagonia — belief as behaviour, not slogan.** Leads with "save our home planet" and demotes
  lifecycle specs to evidence the belief is not a pose. Earns the claim by acting on it, so the
  story is not rented.
- **Mejuri — plant the flag in a behaviour, manufacture repeat.** Created the "everyday fine
  jewellery" lane on a self-purchase belief, demoted material to proof, and built a weekly
  ritual plus a loyalty layer so repeat was engineered, not hoped for. Own a behaviour, not a
  product, and give the retention story a retention machine.

The through-line: **put a belief where the spec currently sits, and let proof support it rather
than lead.** Most brands that feel soulless are selling proof and calling it a story.

## Cautionary exemplars

Worth citing for what not to do.

- **Claiming the words is not owning the position.** Brands that buy the loudest category
  language ("everyday", "affordable", "lightweight") without a differentiated reason-why get
  out-distributed or out-spent by incumbents. Saturated category language is open on commodity
  and closed on meaning. The defensible claim is a specific reason a specific person chooses
  this brand, not the generic category promise.
- **A traffic engine is not a belief.** Brands that scale on paid acquisition or a distribution
  hack, then confuse that engine for a brand, tend to halve or collapse when the engine gets
  expensive. Positioning and demand are different levers.

## The anchor set for the brand in play

Assemble this per engagement and keep it in the brand brief or alongside it. Keep it short —
three to six references beats a mood board of thirty.

- **Keystone (the one to measure against):** the single closest direction reference. Everything
  else is judged relative to it.
- **Direction references:** two to four brands the brand could credibly move toward. For each:
  belief, where the spec sits, founder-story handling, one to steal, one to avoid.
- **Ceiling references:** one or two taste bars. Note what is admirable and what is out of
  budget.
- **Anti-references:** the specific brands, tropes and clichés this brand must not resemble.
  Name them, so "don't be generic" becomes "don't be that".
