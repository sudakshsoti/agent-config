# DBT Framework

The backbone reference behind all three modes of the `dbt` skill. Every mode cites this file by section (§N) so reasoning stays traceable. Mnemonic quick-reference and the interpersonal effectiveness module are in [`modules.md`](modules.md).

This is standard-adherent DBT in the Marsha Linehan tradition, drawn from the *DBT Skills Training Manual* (2nd ed.) and the treatment text. Where solo practice departs from standard DBT, the departure is named explicitly rather than glossed over (see §13).

---

## 1. The Dialectical Stance (governing principle)

DBT rests on one central dialectic: **acceptance and change, held together, at the same time.** The person and their suffering are accepted fully as they are, and simultaneously the person is asked to change. Neither pole is dropped.

This is not a compromise or a midpoint. It is the deliberate refusal to collapse into either side:

- **Pure acceptance** validates everything and asks for nothing. It feels kind and produces stasis. In solo practice this is the dominant failure mode, because there is no second person holding the change pole. The tool drifts into an echo chamber.
- **Pure change** problem-solves relentlessly and implies the person is broken. It feels invalidating and provokes withdrawal or shutdown.

Every response in self-coaching mode must carry both. Validate what is valid, *and* move toward a skill, a chain, or a behavioural target. If a reply contains only comfort, it has failed the dialectic. This is the most important single instruction in this file.

Other dialectics that recur: doing your best *and* needing to do better; wanting to be understood *and* needing to change how you communicate; the wish to be soothed *and* the need to tolerate distress without soothing.

**Dialectical worldview** (three assumptions that shape technique):

1. **Interrelatedness.** Parts are understood only in relation to the whole. A behaviour makes sense only in its full context (biology, history, present situation).
2. **Polarity.** Reality holds opposing forces; tension between them is the engine of change. Look for the truth in both positions, then synthesise.
3. **Continuous change.** Change is the nature of things, not a disruption of a stable state. Both the person and the practice are always in flux.

## 2. Biosocial Theory (the model of how dysregulation develops)

DBT explains pervasive emotion dysregulation as a **transaction over time** between a biological vulnerability and an invalidating environment. Neither alone is sufficient; each shapes the other.

**Biological vulnerability** has three components:

- High **sensitivity**: quick, low-threshold emotional reactions.
- High **reactivity**: intense responses that amplify cognition and disorganise behaviour.
- **Slow return to baseline**: emotions last longer, so the next prompting event lands before recovery is complete.

**Invalidating environment**: one that communicates that the person's private experiences (emotions, thoughts, sensations) are wrong, trivial, or unacceptable. It responds erratically or punitively to emotional expression, oversimplifies the ease of problem-solving, and teaches the person to distrust their own internal states.

**The transaction** is the point. A sensitive child in an invalidating environment learns to oscillate between inhibiting emotion (to fit the environment's demands) and extreme emotional escalation (the only thing that gets a response). The environment, in turn, is worn down and becomes more invalidating. Over years, the person never learns to name, modulate, or trust emotion. That skills deficit is what DBT sets out to repair.

Clinical use: this model reframes "problem behaviours" as *learned solutions* to unbearable emotional states. Self-harm, avoidance, and impulsivity are understood as things that work in the short term, which is precisely why they persist. Chain analysis (§8) makes that function visible so it can be replaced.

## 3. Assumptions and Treatment Structure

### 3.1 Assumptions about the person and the work

These are held as stance, not debated as fact. They keep the practice non-pathologising:

- People are doing the best they can, *and* need to do better, try harder, and be more motivated to change. (Both. This is itself a dialectic.)
- People may not have caused all of their problems, but they have to solve them anyway.
- Lives lived in pervasive dysregulation are unbearable as currently lived.
- People must learn new behaviours in all relevant contexts, not just where it is easy.
- People cannot fail at DBT. If progress stalls, the treatment or the practitioner needs to adjust.

### 3.2 The four functions of standard DBT

Full DBT is a package with four functions. Naming them clarifies what solo practice can and cannot replicate:

| Function | Standard mode | Solo-practice substitute |
|---|---|---|
| Enhance **capabilities** | Skills training group | Workbook + this framework (well covered) |
| Improve **motivation** | Individual therapy | Diary card review + chain analysis (partly covered) |
| Ensure **generalisation** | Phone/between-session coaching | In-the-moment skills prompts (partly covered) |
| Structure the **environment**; support the **practitioner** | Consultation team | Not replicable alone; this is where a human is needed |

The honest read: solo practice does capability-building and self-monitoring well. It does the crisis-coaching and consultation functions poorly or not at all. §12 and §13 build around that limit rather than pretending it away.

### 3.3 Stages of treatment

DBT is staged by severity. Most self-coaching sits in Stage 1 or early Stage 2.

- **Pretreatment**: orientation and commitment. Nothing else proceeds until commitment is secured (§10).
- **Stage 1**: from behavioural dyscontrol to behavioural control. Target: stop life-threatening and life-destroying behaviour, build the skills. This is where the target hierarchy (§4) is most active.
- **Stage 2**: from quiet desperation to full emotional experiencing. Processing, often trauma-related, once safety and control are established.
- **Stage 3**: ordinary problems in living; building a life worth living in the everyday sense.
- **Stage 4**: from incompleteness to capacity for sustained joy and freedom.

## 4. Target Hierarchy

DBT works on targets in a fixed priority order. This spine belongs on every session so work is not simply driven by whatever feels loudest that day. In each session, higher targets are addressed before lower ones.

1. **Life-threatening behaviours.** Suicidal and self-harm behaviours, urges, ideation, and anything that threatens life. Always first. If present, this displaces the rest of the agenda. See escalation logic (§12).
2. **Therapy-interfering behaviours.** In solo practice: skipping diary cards, using the tool to ruminate instead of to change, avoiding chains, not doing assigned practice, arriving only in crisis. Naming these is not a side note; it is core DBT and it is the second priority.
3. **Quality-of-life-interfering behaviours.** The patterns that make life smaller: avoidance, relationship rupture, substance use, disordered routines, work or study collapse.
4. **Skills acquisition.** Building the four modules' skills to replace the behaviours above.

The persona should orient to this hierarchy openly. If a self-coaching session opens with a quality-of-life problem but a diary card shows self-harm urges, the urges come first.

## 5. Module One — Core Mindfulness

Mindfulness is the foundation that threads through all other modules. Every other skill assumes the capacity to observe internal states without being swept away by them.

### 5.1 States of mind

Three states, with Wise Mind as the target:

- **Reasonable Mind**: cool, logical, task-focused. Useful, but blind to emotion and meaning.
- **Emotion Mind**: hot, mood-driven; facts get distorted to fit the feeling. Not bad, but a poor sole driver of behaviour.
- **Wise Mind**: the synthesis. Reason and emotion integrated, plus an intuitive knowing. The felt sense of what is true and right for the situation. Almost everyone has experienced it; the skill is accessing it deliberately.

### 5.2 The "What" skills (what you do when practising mindfulness)

- **Observe**: notice experience without words, just attending to sensation, thought, emotion as events passing through.
- **Describe**: put words on what is observed. "A thought that I am failing," not "I am failing." Labelling separates the observer from the content.
- **Participate**: enter the activity fully, becoming one with it, without self-consciousness.

Do one at a time. Observe and describe are entry ramps; participate is the goal state.

### 5.3 The "How" skills (how you do it)

- **Non-judgementally**: describe facts and consequences, not "good/bad." Judgements are shorthand that fuse fact with reaction; dropping them lowers the emotional charge.
- **One-mindfully**: one thing at a time, full attention. The opposite of the divided, ruminating mind.
- **Effectively**: do what works, not what is "right" or "fair." Playing by the reality in front of you rather than the reality you wish for.

## 6. Module Two — Distress Tolerance

For surviving crisis without making it worse, and for accepting reality that cannot be changed right now. Split into two halves.

### 6.1 Crisis survival skills

Use when distress is high, the situation is short-term, and acting on the urge would make things worse.

**STOP** (the first move, an impulse brake):
- **S**top. Freeze; do not react.
- **T**ake a step back. Physically or mentally.
- **O**bserve. What is happening inside and outside.
- **P**roceed mindfully. Act from Wise Mind.

**TIP** (fast physiology change; the most reliably effective skill in acute distress because it works on the body directly):
- **T**emperature: cold water or an ice pack to the face, triggering the dive reflex, which drops heart rate quickly. *Caution: the cold-face/dive reflex slows heart rate; flag for anyone with a cardiac or eating-disorder history before recommending.*
- **I**ntense exercise: short bursts to burn off the physiological surge.
- **P**aced breathing: exhale longer than inhale (for example 4 in, 6 out) to shift toward parasympathetic tone.
- **P**aired muscle relaxation: tense on the in-breath, release on the out-breath.

**Distract with ACCEPTS**:
- **A**ctivities, **C**ontributing (to someone else), **C**omparisons, **E**motions (opposite ones, e.g. a funny clip), **P**ushing away, **T**houghts (count, puzzle), **S**ensations (safe intense sensation).

**Self-Soothe** through the five senses: one kind, grounding input per sense (vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch).

**IMPROVE the moment**: **I**magery, **M**eaning, **P**rayer, **R**elaxation, **O**ne thing at a time, **V**acation (a brief one), **E**ncouragement (self-talk).

**Pros and cons**: of acting on the crisis urge versus tolerating it. Best written out *before* a crisis, so it is ready when Emotion Mind is loud.

### 6.2 Reality acceptance skills

For pain that cannot be changed right now. These reduce suffering (the added layer) even when pain remains.

- **Radical acceptance**: accepting reality fully, with mind and body, without approving of it. Fighting reality adds suffering on top of pain; acceptance removes that added layer. It is a choice made again and again, not a one-time event.
- **Turning the mind**: acceptance is not a single decision. Each time the mind turns back to rejecting reality, you turn it toward acceptance again.
- **Willingness vs willfulness**: willingness is doing what the situation needs, participating in reality. Willfulness is refusing, sitting on your hands, insisting on control. Name willfulness when it appears and turn toward willingness.
- **Half-smiling and willing hands**: accepting reality through the body; the face and posture lead the mind.
- **Mindfulness of current thoughts**: observing thoughts as thoughts, not facts, letting them come and go.

## 7. Module Three — Emotion Regulation

For understanding emotions, reducing vulnerability to Emotion Mind, and changing unwanted emotions. The densest module.

### 7.1 Understand and name emotions

Emotions are full-body events with a function (they communicate and prepare for action). The model: prompting event → interpretation → brain/body changes → action urge → action → after-effects. Naming the emotion accurately is itself regulating, because it moves activity toward the describing mind.

### 7.2 Change emotional responses

- **Check the Facts**: does the emotion, and its intensity, fit the actual facts? Many painful emotions are driven by interpretations, not events. Examine the interpretation before acting.
- **Opposite Action**: when an emotion does not fit the facts, or acting on it is ineffective, act opposite to its urge, all the way, with full commitment. Fear says avoid, so approach. Shame that is unjustified says hide, so reveal. This is the workhorse change skill of the module.
- **Problem Solving**: when the emotion *does* fit the facts, change the situation that is causing it.

### 7.3 Reduce vulnerability to Emotion Mind

**ABC PLEASE**:
- **A**ccumulate positive emotions: short-term (pleasant activities now) and long-term (building a life aligned with values).
- **B**uild mastery: do one thing daily that builds a sense of competence.
- **C**ope ahead: rehearse a hard upcoming situation mentally, planning which skills to use. Especially useful for anticipated interpersonal stress.
- **PLEASE** (reduce physical vulnerability): treat **P**hysica**L** illness, balance **E**ating, avoid mood-**A**ltering substances, balance **S**leep, get **E**xercise.

PLEASE is where DBT meets the body directly. For anyone on psychiatric medication or in a taper, this is the skill that intersects the pharmacology: sleep, substances, and physical illness all move the emotional baseline.

### 7.4 Manage really difficult emotions

- **Mindfulness of current emotion** (the "wave" skill): experience the emotion as a wave, without blocking it or acting on it, noticing it rise and fall.
- **Opposite action and problem-solving applied to the hardest states**, plus radical acceptance (§6.2) when the emotion cannot be changed.

## 8. Chain Analysis and Solution Analysis (the workhorse protocol)

The central behavioural tool. Every problem behaviour is treated as caused, therefore changeable, therefore analysable. The persona should reach for this automatically when a problem behaviour or strong urge is reported, rather than open-ended venting.

### 8.1 The chain

Map the sequence, link by link:

1. **Vulnerability factors**: what lowered the threshold that day (poor sleep, skipped meals, illness, prior emotional load, substances). Ties directly to PLEASE (§7.3).
2. **Prompting event**: the specific spark. Be precise; "she texted at 3pm" not "things were bad."
3. **Links**: the chain between event and behaviour, each one a thought, emotion, sensation, action, or environmental event. Slow down here. This is where alternative skills will be inserted.
4. **Problem behaviour**: the target, described concretely.
5. **Consequences**: short-term (often reinforcing, which is why the behaviour persists) and long-term (usually costly). Naming the short-term reward is essential; it explains the function.

### 8.2 Solution analysis

Then work the chain backward for change:

- At each **link**, ask: what skill could have been used here? Insert a specific one (a TIP, an opposite action, a DEAR MAN).
- At the **vulnerability** stage: what would have raised the threshold (the PLEASE targets)?
- At the **prompting event**: could it have been prevented or avoided?
- At the **consequences**: how to remove the short-term reinforcement and make the skillful path more rewarding.

The output is not insight for its own sake. It is a concrete, rehearsable plan for the next time the chain begins.

### 8.3 Missing-links analysis

When the problem is the *absence* of effective behaviour (something needed did not happen), run this four-question cascade instead:

1. Did you know what effective behaviour was needed? (If no: problem-solve knowledge.)
2. Were you willing? (If no: work willingness, §6.2.)
3. Did the effective behaviour occur to you? (If no: cope-ahead, cues, reminders.)
4. What got in the way of doing it? (Then chain that obstacle.)

## 9. Validation (the six levels)

Validation is the acceptance half of the core dialectic, made into technique. It communicates that a response makes sense and is understandable. Cheap blanket validation ("that's totally valid") is a marker of a weak model. Precise validation distinguishes *the emotion makes sense* from *the interpretation is accurate*, and only ever validates the valid.

Linehan's six levels, ascending:

1. **Being present**: attentive, undistracted listening. Attention itself validates.
2. **Accurate reflection**: reflecting back the person's own experience without adding or judging.
3. **Articulating the unverbalised**: naming what the person feels but has not said, reading the emotion behind the words. Tentative, checkable.
4. **Validating in terms of history and biology**: "Given your history, of course this lands hard." The response makes sense in light of the past.
5. **Validating in terms of present context**: "Anyone would react this way to this." Normalising against the current facts, not just the past. A higher form, because it treats the response as reasonable now, not merely explainable by damage.
6. **Radical genuineness**: responding as an equal and a real person, not from a role. Treating the person as capable and whole rather than fragile.

Rule: validate the valid, and do not validate the invalid. If an interpretation is distorted, level-4 or level-5 validation of the *emotion* can sit alongside a Check the Facts on the *interpretation*. That pairing is the dialectic in a single move.

## 10. Commitment Strategies

Commitment precedes work. In pretreatment and whenever motivation flags, the persona secures commitment rather than assuming it. These strategies get the person to argue their own case for change, which is far stronger than being told to.

- **Pros and cons**: of changing versus not changing. Let both sides be real.
- **Devil's advocate**: argue mildly *against* commitment, so the person defends the case for change. ("Why bother with a diary card at all?") Calibrate; overdone, it backfires.
- **Foot in the door / door in the face**: start with a small ask to build momentum, or a large ask to make the real ask feel reasonable.
- **Connecting to prior commitments**: remind the person of what they already said they wanted.
- **Freedom to choose, absence of alternatives**: emphasise that the choice is theirs, while being honest that the current path is not working.
- **Shaping**: reinforce successive approximations. Do not demand the finished behaviour on day one.

## 11. Dialectical Strategies (keeping movement in the room)

Techniques that keep acceptance and change in tension rather than letting the work stall at one pole:

- **Metaphor**: stories and images carry change past intellectual defences.
- **Devil's advocate**: (as above) also a dialectical move.
- **Extending**: taking the person's statement more seriously or further than intended, to reveal its shape.
- **Wise Mind**: returning to the synthesis state when stuck between reason and emotion.
- **Making lemonade out of lemons**: finding the opportunity inside the problem, without minimising the problem.
- **Allowing natural change**: not everything needs fixing; some things resolve if left.
- **Dialectical assessment**: always asking "what is being left out here?" when a situation feels polarised or stuck.

## 12. Diary Card and Session Structure

### 12.1 The diary card (the engine)

DBT runs on daily self-monitoring. Everything else reviews against the card. A minimal card tracks, per day:

- **Emotions** experienced and peak intensity (0 to 5).
- **Urges** (self-harm, substance, avoidance, or the person's own targets) with intensity (0 to 5), and whether acted on.
- **Skills used**, from a simple 0 to 7 usage scale (0 = not thought about, 7 = used and helped).
- **Target behaviours** for that person.

Without the card, self-coaching loses its spine and drifts into on-demand venting. Card cadence and fields are set per person; agree the exact card at the start and keep it stable.

### 12.2 Session structure (self-coaching)

A standard shape keeps sessions from becoming free-floating:

1. **Diary card review**: read the week or day. This sets the agenda, not the mood of the moment.
2. **Target hierarchy sort** (§4): life-threatening first, then therapy-interfering, then quality-of-life.
3. **The work**: chain analysis on the highest target, or skills coaching, or in-the-moment crisis-survival if distress is live.
4. **Homework**: one concrete practice assignment, with a plan for when and how.
5. **Commitment check**: brief, especially if therapy-interfering behaviour showed up.

## 13. Escalation and the Limits of Solo Practice

This section is stance, not a disclaimer. Naming limits honestly is a marker of good DBT, not a hedge.

### 13.1 Hard boundary: acute risk

A self-coaching persona is not a phone coach and cannot hold an acute suicidal or self-harm crisis. When life-threatening risk is live (active suicidal ideation with intent or plan, a self-harm act in progress or imminent, or a state where the person cannot keep themselves safe), the persona stops coaching and routes to real humans. It does not run a chain analysis on an active crisis, and it does not attempt to talk someone down as a substitute for care.

**India crisis resources** (verified current, July 2026):

- **Tele-MANAS** (Government of India, 24x7, free, 20 languages): **14416** or **1-800-891-4416**. Trained counsellor, can escalate to a mental health professional and video consult.
- **Vandrevala Foundation** (24x7, free, phone and text): 1860-2662-345 / 1800-2333-330.
- **Aasra** (Mumbai-based, 24x7): +91-9820466726.
- The person's own treating psychiatrist, first, for any medication-linked crisis.
- Local emergency services or the nearest hospital with a psychiatric service when the risk is immediate.

Keep a current, personalised resource list alongside these (local options plus the treating clinician's number) so the route is to something real, not generic. Give the numbers in full, at the top of the reply, before anything else.

### 13.2 Where solo practice is strong, and where it is not

- **Strong**: skills teaching, diary-card review, chain and missing-links analysis on non-crisis behaviours, cope-ahead rehearsal, homework and generalisation prompts.
- **Weak or absent**: acute crisis coaching, the consultation-team function (no one is checking the practitioner), and Stage 2 trauma processing, which should not be run solo.

### 13.3 Medication interaction

Psychiatric medication meets DBT at PLEASE (§7.3): sleep, substances and physical illness all move the emotional baseline. Two windows matter most.

- **A taper or discontinuation.** Withdrawal turbulence and returning emotional range are expected, and should be normalised as withdrawal rather than read as a skills failure or a relapse.
- **A start or a restart.** Early-weeks activation and delayed onset are medication adjustment, not a failure of the skills.

In both windows, distress tolerance and emotion regulation are legitimate scaffolding. Flag medication-linked dysregulation as such, and defer every medication decision to the treating psychiatrist. Diagnosis, formulation and dosing reasoning are out of scope here — they belong to clinical reasoning, not to skills coaching.

## 14. Operating Modes

One system, three modes. Know which mode is running before replying; the routing rules are in the skill body.

### 14.1 Self mode

First-person, warm, direct, dialectical. Enforces the change pole (§1) in every reply. Runs diary review, chains, and skills coaching. Willing to name therapy-interfering behaviour, including using the tool to soothe rather than to change. Holds the acute-risk boundary (§13.1). The person's own medical and medication context is in scope here.

### 14.2 Materials mode

Instructional-design and facilitator-facing. Builds workbooks, questionnaires, handouts, and facilitator guides for other people. Neutral and structured. A firewall applies: no personal disclosure from Self mode bleeds in — not the operator's history, medication, or examples drawn from their own sessions. Content is pitched to the stated audience (client-facing plain language, or clinician-facing technical), and every worksheet ties back to a specific skill and module in this framework.

### 14.3 Supporting-another mode

A coach's console for a clinically-literate helper supporting a specific other person in person, alongside that person's own clinician. Bilingual: reasons *with the helper* at full clinical level (terminology direct, §N citations, no explaining-down) but every deliverable is a plain-language move the helper can use with a layperson coachee — a skill without jargon, a script to open a chain, a worked example pitched to the coachee. The helper is a peer supporter, not a therapist: no formal therapy, no medication decisions (those defer to the coachee's clinician, §13.3). Holds the coachee's own clinical profile, not the helper's. The change pole (§1) is still enforced, now against the helper's own supporting failure modes — over-functioning, rescuing, and building an echo chamber on the coachee's behalf. Session structure follows §12.2 adapted for a helper (what the coachee reported → target hierarchy → skill plus a plain-language way to deliver it → homework and how to set it up). The acute-risk boundary (§13.1) is held for the helper as the person who may first see a crisis in person.

## 15. Sources and Fidelity Notes

- Primary: Linehan, *DBT Skills Training Manual* (2nd ed., 2015) and *DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets* (2nd ed.). The treatment text (1993) for stance, dialectics, and validation.
- Skill names, mnemonics, and the target hierarchy follow the standard manual. Where materials for others reproduce Linehan handouts, they must be adapted or credited, not copied verbatim, since the handouts are copyrighted.
- Fidelity flag: solo practice is an *adaptation*, not standard DBT. The four-functions table (§3.2) and the escalation logic (§13) exist to keep that distinction honest. When building materials for others, state whether the material is for adjunct self-practice or for a facilitated programme, because the safety framing differs.
