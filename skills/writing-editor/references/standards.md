# Standards and lenses

Consult before giving draft feedback, or when naming what a piece is missing.

## Four-writer lenses

Named lenses, not decoration. Name the writer when applying the lens.

### Munro — compression and emotional economy

**Apply when:** the draft overexplains, narrates feelings, or runs long on interior monologue.
**Phrasing:** "Munro would cut this to a single gesture and let the reader do the rest — which gesture?"
**Test:** can the paragraph be half as long and twice as felt?

### Burkeman — philosophical ideas landing in daily life

**Apply when:** the writing is abstract, about "the human condition", or generalising.
**Phrasing:** "Burkeman would anchor this in a Tuesday afternoon, not the human condition."
**Test:** is there a specific weekday, room, or object the idea touches down on?

### Chayka — cultural criticism of ordinary things

**Apply when:** the writer dismisses an idea as too small or too domestic.
**Phrasing:** "Chayka wrote a book about the colour beige. The smallness isn't the problem — your treatment of it is."
**Test:** is the ordinary subject being taken seriously as a subject?

### Bennett — dry observational warmth, comedy doing emotional work

**Apply when:** a feeling is overexplained, or the ending is trying to summarise.
**Phrasing:** "Bennett would end on the throwaway line and trust the reader."
**Test:** is the warmth showing in the observation, or being announced?

## Six standards

A checklist for reading drafts. Name the principle when something is missing.

1. **Specificity over generality.** At least one image, object, or moment the reader can see. If you cannot picture it, it is not earning the page.
2. **Emotional honesty without sentimentality.** Say what you mean. Do not perform vulnerability — that reads as fake even when the feeling is real.
3. **Earned complexity.** Start concrete, let abstraction emerge. Never start with the thesis.
4. **Tonal control.** Sound like one person talking, not like "writing". If a sentence sounds like a Medium post, it is one.
5. **Endings that resonate, not conclude.** No bow. Munro ends mid-gesture; Bennett ends on a throwaway line that is not throwaway.
6. **Economy.** Every sentence does at least one job. Most do two.
