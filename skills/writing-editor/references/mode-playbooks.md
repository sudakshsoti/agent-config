# Mode playbooks

Operational detail behind the four modes. Consult on entering a mode when a worked
example or a specific prompt would help.

## §spark — idea generation

### Reading-to-writing exercises (10 minutes each)

Reading is the primary creative input. Convert it into writing fuel with one of these:

- **The disagreement.** "Take the piece you just read. Write the sentence you wish the author had written instead."
- **The argument.** "Summarise the piece in one sentence. Now write your version of that sentence."
- **The rewrite.** "Find the weakest paragraph in that piece. Write the version you'd have written."
- **The 200.** "What did you disagree with? Write 200 words about it. No essay scaffolding."

### Seed guardrails

Seeds must be specific and angled. Format: `[concrete subject] as [angled lens]`.

- Good: "Self-hosting as a form of domesticity."
- Good: "Designing for people who don't read instructions, written by someone who doesn't read instructions."
- Bad: "Write about self-hosting." (no angle)
- Bad: "Productivity in the modern age." (no specificity)

### Mining the day job

Whatever the writer does for a living gives them a view most readers do not have. Surface
essay material from it when they are stuck. Adapt these to their actual work:

- "You spend your days doing X. That's an essay."
- "What did someone do today that your system didn't expect?"
- "What's the gap between what you built and what's actually being used?"

### Match the container

Not every idea is a 2,000-word essay. Help name the right format:

- 400 words: an observation, a notebook entry, a paragraph publishable as-is.
- 1,000 words: a single argument with one example.
- 2,000+ words: only if there are two ideas that need each other.

If a 400-word piece is being inflated to 2,000, say so.

## §draft — structural feedback

### Opening template

1. **First question, always:** "Did you read this aloud?" If not, suggest it before any line-level feedback.
2. **Word count plus verdict.** "This is 1,800 words and the real piece is 900 of them."
3. **Identify the single strongest paragraph.** The writer needs to know what is already working before they can cut.
4. **Name the real subject.** If the piece says it is about X but is actually about Y, say so.

### Shorthand

Use the named flags defined in SKILL.md: **CLOSER**, **SHOW**, **CUT**, **LAND**, **REAL END**.

### Scan for, in order

- Hedging, qualifying, retreating into cleverness instead of saying something direct.
- Abstraction where there could be a specific image.
- Tonal slips — sentences that sound like content, not like a person.
- The real ending. (Usually two paragraphs before the written one.)

### Do not rewrite their sentences

Quote the weak passage, explain what is not working, let them fix it. One rewrite only if
they explicitly ask.

## §ship — publishing

### Title generation

Three options, in this shape:

- **Literal** — names the subject directly.
- **Angled** — names the subject through the lens.
- **Unexpected** — only obvious in retrospect.

### Opening-line audit

Does the first sentence earn the second? If the piece buries the hook, name where the real
opening is.

### Platform fit

If the publishing platform is established in the conversation, check length and tone
against it. If not, ask before giving length or format advice.

### The done-enough call

If the writer is polishing to avoid publishing, name it. "This is ready. The next draft
won't be better, just different. Ship it."

### After publishing

One question: "What surprised you about writing that one?" That is the seed for the next
piece.

## §push — momentum

### Constrained prompts (pick one)

- "Write about your commute home yesterday in exactly 300 words. No metaphors."
- "Write 200 words about the last thing that annoyed you. Don't think about whether it's an essay."
- "Write the email you'd send to a friend describing the X you mentioned. That's the draft."
- "Take a piece you read this week. Write 200 words disagreeing with it."

### Call the pattern

When the writer is deflecting into more ideation:

- "You've opened three conversations this month and generated ideas in all of them. You haven't written any. The bottleneck isn't ideas."
- "You've been talking about writing for 20 minutes. The clock is the problem, not the prompt."

### Anchors

Mine the current moment for prompts — the season, the heat, air quality, a festival, a
current event, something that happened to the writer this week. "Write about what 42°C
does to a commute" beats "write about summer".

### Do not be motivational

Motivation is rarely the problem; permission and momentum are. Never say "just write".
Always give a specific prompt with a constraint.
